How this works

The name is Latin: certiorem facere, to make someone better informed.

Certior shows how outlets across the political spectrum and around the world cover the same event. It is the same feed for everyone, with no accounts and no personalisation, so it can't learn to show you only what you already agree with.

It is not neutral, though. Every choice below shapes what you see, so here they are.

Which outlets

Around 50 YouTube channels that publish in English, chosen by hand. The full list is public. English only leaves out most local-language coverage, and Latin America is thinly covered.

Left, Center and Right

These come from Media Bias/Fact Check. They rate the whole outlet, not the individual clip, so a clip can differ from its label. Left also holds the outlets they rate left-center, and Right those they rate right-center; each clip shows its outlet's own rating, such as Lean Left. The scale is a US left-right scale, and it fits US politics best.

That is why outlets a government funds have their own group, State-funded, instead of a place on the scale: the US scale had put Saudi state TV next to Fox. The rule is YouTube's own notice on the outlet's channel: when it says the outlet "is funded in whole or in part by" a government (or the European Union), the outlet is State-funded. Public broadcasters that YouTube describes as a "public broadcast service", such as the BBC, DW or TRT, stay on the scale. Outlets Media Bias/Fact Check doesn't rate appear as Not rated. State-funded and Not rated clips come after Left, Center and Right when you swipe.

Funding notices

The notices on some clips use YouTube's own wording about who funds or runs the outlet. With videos on, YouTube's player shows them; Compare Headlines always has them in full.

Grouping clips into stories

An AI model compares clip titles, and clips with very similar titles about the same thing become one story. It makes mistakes: sometimes a clip lands in the wrong story, and sometimes coverage of one event is split up. In a test on 200 deliberately hard pairs of real clips, labelled by Claude rather than an independent reviewer, about 90% of the pairs it grouped were right and it found about 85% of real matches. That is not its accuracy across the whole feed.

Swiping sideways

Every outlet that covered a story stays in it. Stories open on Center unless you choose otherwise in Settings. Each side's clips sit together, in the order of the coloured dots under Left, Center and Right: swiping sideways shows the next outlet on the same side, then moves on to the next side, and outlets off that scale come last. Inside a side, the headline least like the others comes first. Tap a side up top to jump to it, or hold the dots and slide along them to pick a clip. A clip plays again until you swipe.

On a computer, the arrow keys do what swiping does: up and down for stories, left and right for outlets. A wide window also keeps the headlines of the story you're on next to it.

What comes first

  • Stories covered by more outlets, more regions and more sides rank higher, and newer beats older.
  • Stories you can compare across sides come before one-sided ones.
  • At most one story in every four is US domestic politics, because every region covers it and it would otherwise fill the feed. Word matching decides this, and it sometimes gets it wrong.

Recap

Recap lists each day's biggest stories for the last week, the ones the most outlets here covered (at least three), and the stories that began since you last opened the app. Your last visit is only stored on your device. Under each story is the first headline from each side, so no single outlet's framing stands in for the story. Tapping one opens it with the rest of that list, and Back returns to Recap and then to your place in the feed. Stories older than two days open on YouTube.

Who covered it first

For a story that began in the last 12 hours, Compare Headlines also lists the outlets in the order their clips went up, and how long the story took to reach other regions. That is when the clip went up on YouTube, which can come after an outlet's live or written reporting, so it says who posted first here, not who broke the news. Older stories don't get it: by then, the oldest clip still in the app's two-day window says little about who was first.

Words each side used more

When there are any, the bottom of Recap shows words that Left outlets used more than Right ones in their headlines, and the reverse, over the last week. Only stories both sides covered count, so it compares how they worded the same events rather than which events they chose. Each outlet counts once per story, so one busy channel can't speak for its side. A word is shown only when the difference is too big to be chance (a z-score of at least 3 in a weighted log-odds test) and at least 3 outlets on that side used it. Often nothing passes, and then the section doesn't appear.

Tabs

Stories are sorted into tabs by words in their titles, and one story can sit under several. Going by a sample we read, roughly 8 in 10 are in the right place, and some real stories in each topic are missed. Some outlets only cover one subject, like Vogue or WIRED, and always appear under that tab. Anything that matches nothing appears only under All.

World

World groups stories by where each outlet is based, and starts with the ones no other outlet in this app has. Those stories aren't always about that country, and countries with few outlets here will have less to show. Take Me Somewhere picks a country at random.

Your mix

This shows how many clips you've watched from each side in the last 7 days. It is calculated on your device and never leaves it, and it doesn't change what stories you get.

A side that's grayed out

If Left, Center or Right is grayed out, no outlet from that side in this app covered the story. Outlets not in this app may well have.

Search

Search matches the words in titles from the last two days. It doesn't understand meaning, so related stories that don't use your words won't appear.
